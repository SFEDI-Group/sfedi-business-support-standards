# SFEDI Business Support Standards

Version 1.9, November 2026.

## About these Standards

The SFEDI Business Support Standards set out what good business support looks like: what a professional supporting businesses needs to be able to do, and what they need to know and understand to do it well.

They are the evolution of the national standards for business support that SFEDI first developed and published between 2007 and 2010: the Business Support, Business Information and Introduction to Enterprise Support standards. SFEDI's standards were updated in 2013 as the national Business and Enterprise Support standards. Together, they have underpinned qualifications, recruitment, appraisal, CPD and the contracting of business support for nearly two decades. This edition keeps what has stood the test of practice, removes duplication and dated references, and adds what business support professionals now need, including AI and digital adoption, sustainability, data protection, today's finance landscape, working with neurodivergent and disabled clients, and evidencing impact.

References to business throughout should be taken to mean all kinds and forms of enterprise, including self-employment, sole traders, family businesses, social enterprises and co-operatives.

## Who they are for

- Business advisers, mentors, coaches, consultants and trainers who support people starting, running and growing businesses
- Professionals who serve small businesses, such as accountants, bookkeepers, solicitors and bank staff
- People who design, commission and manage business support programmes
- Training and CPD providers who want to align their programmes to a recognised benchmark
- Employers who recruit, develop and appraise business support staff

## How the Standards are structured

| Part | What it covers |
|---|---|
| Professional behaviours (B1 to B11) | The behaviours expected in every area of business support. They apply to every Standard |
| Core Standards (C1 to C8) | The main functions of a business support professional, which every practitioner is expected to meet |
| Specialist Standards (S1 to S27) | Areas of specialist practice that build on the Core Standards |
| Business Information Standards (I1 to I4) | Information, enquiry and front-line roles, such as helplines, enquiry teams and first-point-of-contact navigators |
| Introduction to Business Support Standards (E1 to E5) | For people thinking of working in business support or new to it, as a basis for progressing to the Core Standards |

## How to read a Standard

Each Standard follows the format used in the original SFEDI standards:

- **Summary**: what the Standard is about and who it applies to
- **What you need to show**: the performance requirements your practice must meet, coded (for example C1.1)
- **What you need to know and understand**: the knowledge that underpins that practice, coded (for example C1.K1)
- **Evolved from**: the original SFEDI standards it replaces or draws on

Specialist Standards also say who they are for. Codes start with C for Core, S for Specialist, I for Business Information and E for Introduction (entry).

## How the Standards are used

**By professionals**, to assess their own practice, plan CPD and show clients and employers what they can do.

**By employers and commissioners**, to write job descriptions, recruit and develop staff, and specify the quality of support they expect from providers.

**Through Specialist Certification.** Professionals can be certified against a Specialist Standard through SFEDI Specialist Programmes, CPD or workshops.

**Through SFEDI Specialist Programmes.** Training and CPD providers can map their workshops and programmes to the Specialist Standards and apply to SFEDI for approval. SFEDI then certificates learners who complete them.

Every SFEDI certificate can be verified online.

## Mapping a programme to the Standards

Every requirement has its own code so that programmes and evidence can be mapped precisely.

- Performance requirements (for example S3.4) describe what a professional does in practice. Evidence for these must come from real client work, or from realistic practice where SFEDI agrees.
- Knowledge requirements (for example S3.K2) can be evidenced through learning and assessment, such as assignments, questions, case studies or professional discussion.
- A SFEDI Specialist Programme states which codes it covers, how it covers them and how learning is checked.
- Professional behaviours are demonstrated through practice and are assumed in every Standard.

## Professional behaviours

Every professional working to these Standards is expected to show the following behaviours. They apply to every Core and Specialist Standard, so they are not repeated in each one.

- B1 Put the client's interests first and act with honesty, integrity and impartiality
- B2 Be open with clients about what you can offer, keep your commitments to them and protect their confidentiality
- B3 Shape your approach around each client's goals, circumstances and ways of working, and show empathy with clients from all walks of life
- B4 Listen carefully, ask searching but even-handed questions, confirm you have understood, and speak and write clearly and simply
- B5 Build trust across the whole of a working relationship, and encourage open, two-way feedback
- B6 Look at the whole picture, gather the information you need, reach well-founded conclusions and offer options you can justify
- B7 Use what you know and have experienced, while staying curious about new ideas, evidence and technology
- B8 Deal with disagreement, resistance, setbacks and uncertainty calmly and constructively
- B9 Know the limits of your competence, and seek or refer to specialist help when a situation goes beyond them
- B10 Treat every client fairly and work inclusively with people from all backgrounds and circumstances
- B11 Keep learning and developing so that your practice keeps improving

## Core Standards

The Core Standards describe the main functions of a business support professional. Every practitioner working to these Standards is expected to meet them, whatever their role or specialism.

### C1 Build and manage professional client relationships

Evolved from: Business Support 1 (Maintain effective business support relationships with clients); Business Information 1 (in part); Introduction to Enterprise Support 1; Business and Enterprise Support (2013) units 1, 3 and 4.

#### Summary

This is about establishing, managing and ending professional relationships with clients, whether you are working with an individual or a team, so that they trust you and the service you represent. It covers agreeing what support will be provided, communicating in the ways that suit each client, building credibility, handling difficulties, keeping accurate records and closing each engagement well. It also covers reaching people who could benefit from support and explaining clearly what you offer.

#### What you need to show

- C1.1 Agree with the client what support you will provide, who is responsible for what, any costs, and what any contract or funding conditions do and do not allow
- C1.2 Keep in touch with each client through the channels that suit them, including face to face, telephone, video and digital channels
- C1.3 Create conversations in which the client, or each member of a client team, feels able to talk openly about the business and the personal matters that affect it
- C1.4 When working with a client team or a family business, establish who does what, what each person needs and how much influence they have
- C1.5 Help the client put into words what they want to achieve, for the business and for themselves
- C1.6 Notice when a client is anxious, under pressure or struggling, talk to them about it with care, and direct them to appropriate personal or specialist support
- C1.7 Build credibility and trust with clients and relevant stakeholders through consistent and reliable delivery
- C1.8 Give constructive feedback, encourage the client to give feedback, and use it to strengthen the relationship
- C1.9 Recognise and manage disagreement or conflict with clients constructively, involving others where this would help
- C1.10 Record client contact, agreed actions and outcomes accurately, promptly and securely, in line with data protection requirements
- C1.11 Check with the client at regular points how well the relationship and the support are working for them, and adjust where needed
- C1.12 Close each engagement in a planned way, agreeing next steps and leaving the door open for the client to come back
- C1.13 Explain clearly to potential clients what support you offer, how it could help them and any costs, in person and online
- C1.14 Reach and engage potential clients through networks, referral partners and other channels, including people less likely to seek support

#### What you need to know and understand

- C1.K1 The interpersonal and communication skills that support effective client work, including active listening, open and probing questions, summarising and reflecting, constructive challenge, negotiation, feedback and handling difficult conversations
- C1.K2 When each way of communicating works best (for example face to face, telephone, video, email, messaging and online platforms), and how to make each accessible
- C1.K3 How to adapt your communication for people of different backgrounds, cultures and communication needs, including neurodivergent people, and how to make reasonable adjustments
- C1.K4 How to contract a support relationship, covering scope, roles, boundaries, costs, timescales and how it will end
- C1.K5 How the personality, competence, role and level of authority of the people you work with can affect a support relationship, and how to work with them
- C1.K6 The eligibility conditions and contractual obligations of the services you deliver, including those attached to public funding
- C1.K7 The signs that a client may need personal support (for example with wellbeing, mental health or personal debt), and where to direct them
- C1.K8 Your organisation's requirements for recording client information, and the data protection rules that apply to client records
- C1.K9 How to prevent, manage and resolve conflict in a support relationship
- C1.K10 How to develop and communicate a clear value proposition for the support you provide
- C1.K11 Approaches to reaching and engaging potential clients, including under-represented groups, and how to judge which work

### C2 Diagnose needs and analyse the business

Evolved from: Business Support 2 (Help the client to analyse the business and decide the way forward); Business Information 1 and 2 (in part); Business and Enterprise Support (2013) units 2, 5 and 8.

#### Summary

This is about helping clients to build a clear, evidence-based picture of their business: where it is, where it is heading and what needs to change. It involves gathering and analysing information, using recognised diagnostic tools, separating the symptoms of problems from their causes and agreeing priorities. Wherever possible you should work in a way that helps clients develop these analytical skills for themselves.

#### What you need to show

- C2.1 Invite the client to share their own view of how the business is performing, where it is heading and what matters to them and its stakeholders
- C2.2 Gather and structure information about the client's business, its market and its operating environment, using appropriate questioning, data and research
- C2.3 Use recognised measures and diagnostic tools with the client to build a shared understanding of where the business stands now
- C2.4 Distinguish between the symptoms and the root causes of business problems
- C2.5 Help the client pinpoint the opportunities open to the business and what is holding its performance back
- C2.6 Help the client weigh up possible changes in terms of their objectives, the risks and benefits, and the resources they can draw on
- C2.7 Encourage the client to set objectives for the short, medium and longer term that are realistic, precise and stretching, for themselves and the business
- C2.8 Develop clear, evidence-based recommendations that support the client's decision making
- C2.9 Agree with the client the priorities and the way forward, and help them record it in a form of plan suited to the business
- C2.10 Help the client involve other stakeholders and build their support for the way forward
- C2.11 Build the client's own ability to analyse their business, so they rely less on outside help over time

#### What you need to know and understand

- C2.K1 Qualitative and quantitative diagnostic techniques (for example SWOT, PESTLE, five forces, business model and value proposition analysis, benchmarking and maturity assessments)
- C2.K2 Key performance indicators and other measures of business performance, and how to read them
- C2.K3 How to find and use reliable sources of business and market information, including official statistics and online data, and how to judge their quality
- C2.K4 Problem definition and root cause analysis techniques
- C2.K5 Techniques for identifying and assessing business risk
- C2.K6 The typical challenges and opportunities for businesses of different sizes and stages, and in different business models (including social enterprises, family businesses, franchising, licensing and distribution agreements)
- C2.K7 How to spot trends and developments likely to affect the client's business (for example customer demand, new technology including AI, regulation, energy and climate, and international trade)
- C2.K8 How to set objectives, and how to structure a business or growth plan that suits the size and needs of the business
- C2.K9 How to map stakeholders and their influence, and how to win their backing
- C2.K10 How to understand a business's culture and what drives, enables and limits the people in it
- C2.K11 How social, cultural and religious values can shape the way people run their businesses

### C3 Support action and change

Evolved from: Business Support 3 (Enable clients to implement changes in their business); Business Support 4 (Encourage clients to act for themselves); Business Support 15 (in part); Introduction to Enterprise Support 4; Business and Enterprise Support (2013) units 8, 9, 10 and 16 (in part).

#### Summary

This is about helping clients turn decisions into action, sustain change and become more capable, confident and independent. It involves action planning, supporting implementation and adapting when circumstances change. It also involves choosing the mode of support that suits the client and the stage of the relationship, whether that is advising, mentoring, coaching, facilitating, training or consultancy.

#### What you need to show

- C3.1 Gauge how ready the client is to make changes, and help them build the commitment to follow through
- C3.2 Draw out the client's doubts about change, including those they have not voiced, and address them with evidence and reasoning
- C3.3 Develop an action plan with the client covering what they want to achieve, the steps, who does what and by when, the resources needed, the risks and how progress will be tracked
- C3.4 Be clear with the client about your part in supporting the plan, and what other support or resources they will need
- C3.5 Select the type of support that best fits the client and the point you have reached together (for example advising, mentoring or coaching), and tell the client when you switch between them
- C3.6 Find out how the client learns best, and work in those ways
- C3.7 Support the client as they put the plan into action and track it, adapting it when circumstances change or things do not go to plan
- C3.8 Help the client work through personal or relationship issues that could block change
- C3.9 Work with the client in ways that build their skills, knowledge, confidence and independence
- C3.10 Sustain the client's momentum by giving feedback and recognising the progress they make

#### What you need to know and understand

- C3.K1 The signs of a client's readiness and commitment to change, and the factors behind them (for example ambivalence, competing priorities and long-standing habits)
- C3.K2 Techniques for planning actions, setting priorities, preparing for contingencies and comparing risks with benefits
- C3.K3 How advising, mentoring, coaching, facilitation, training and consultancy differ, and when each is the right choice
- C3.K4 Styles of consulting intervention (for example acceptant, catalytic, confrontational and prescriptive) and their effects
- C3.K5 How adults learn (for example the experiential learning cycle), and how to adapt your approach to different learning preferences and needs
- C3.K6 How self-doubt, limiting beliefs, weak routines or personal circumstances can hold a client back, and how to help
- C3.K7 How a client's circumstances, including disability, neurodiversity, gender, ethnicity, age, sexual orientation, caring responsibilities and reliance on benefits, can shape their experience of running a business, and the support available
- C3.K8 How to support decisions without taking them over, so that the client stays in control
- C3.K9 How to involve and delegate to others in delivering an action plan

### C4 Navigate, refer and broker support

Evolved from: Business Support 10 (Broker clients to business support services); Business Support 13 (Help the client to critically assess a business support service provider's offer); Business Support 14 (Broker deals with business support service providers); Business Support 15 (Help the client to build confidence and capacity to evaluate and broker their own solutions); Business Support 5 (in part); Business and Enterprise Support (2013) units 4 (in part), 14, 15 and 16. C4 covers finding, explaining and referring to support; S25 (Brokering Specialist Support) covers helping clients specify, select, fund and manage providers in depth.

#### Summary

This is about helping clients, impartially, to find, access and evaluate the support they need from others. It involves knowing the national, local and sector support available, explaining the options, referring clients well and following up, while making sure the client keeps responsibility for their own decisions. Over time it means building the client's ability to do this for themselves.

#### What you need to show

- C4.1 Identify the national, local and sector support relevant to the client's needs, including public programmes, finance, specialist advice and peer networks
- C4.2 Explain the options available to the client, including costs, eligibility and any public subsidy involved
- C4.3 Refer or introduce the client to appropriate services, explaining why, and with the client's consent to share their information
- C4.4 Stay impartial throughout, leaving the choice of provider, and how to use them, with the client
- C4.5 Follow up referrals to check they have met the client's needs, and act on problems within your responsibility
- C4.6 Help the client become confident and capable of finding and judging support for themselves in future
- C4.7 Use feedback from clients and colleagues on providers to improve the quality of future referrals

#### What you need to know and understand

- C4.K1 The current landscape of business support, including national services, local and regional provision, and sector, private and voluntary provision, and how to keep up to date with it
- C4.K2 The sources of finance and funding available for business support and business development
- C4.K3 Referral procedures and eligibility rules, and how to obtain and record consent to share client information
- C4.K4 How to judge whether a referral or another provider's support has met the client's needs
- C4.K5 The records to keep of referrals

### C5 Measure and evidence impact

Evolved from: Business Support 8 (Monitor the performance of the business support service); Business Support 15 (items f to h); Business Support 17 (item h); Business Support 6 (in part); Business and Enterprise Support (2013) units 12 and 16 (in part).

#### Summary

This is about agreeing with clients how success will be judged, reviewing progress and outcomes, and recording and reporting evidence of impact for clients, your organisation, commissioners and funders. It also covers using what you learn to improve the support provided, by you and by your organisation.

#### What you need to show

- C5.1 Agree with the client at the start what success will look like, and when and how you will review progress together
- C5.2 Help the client identify indicators and methods for measuring the impact of the support on their business
- C5.3 At the agreed points, look back with the client at progress against their objectives and plan
- C5.4 Help the client assess the effect of the support on the business, distinguishing it from other influences
- C5.5 Invite the client to tell you about any problems with the support, look into them, and put them right yourself or pass them to whoever can
- C5.6 Record outcomes and impact accurately and consistently, in line with organisational, contractual and data protection requirements
- C5.7 Report evidence of impact clearly to those who need it, including clients, your organisation, commissioners and funders
- C5.8 Report where support may breach legislation, regulations, contractual terms or organisational guidelines
- C5.9 Use findings and feedback to recommend and make improvements to the support provided by you and your organisation

#### What you need to know and understand

- C5.K1 Why monitoring and evaluation matter to clients, organisations, commissioners and funders
- C5.K2 Measures of business outcomes and impact (for example turnover, profit, productivity, jobs, investment, survival, skills and environmental performance) and their limitations
- C5.K3 Methods of collecting evidence of impact, including client feedback, case studies, before-and-after measures and follow-up
- C5.K4 The principles of attribution and additionality, and the limits of what can reasonably be claimed
- C5.K5 How to judge value for money, efficiency, effectiveness and quality in business support
- C5.K6 The monitoring and reporting requirements of the contracts and funding programmes you work on
- C5.K7 Approaches to comparing support services with others, and using the results to improve them
- C5.K8 How to handle complaints and problems with the support provided

### C6 Apply current business knowledge

Evolved from: Business Support 9 (Apply your knowledge and experience of business and its core functions); Business Information 8; Introduction to Enterprise Support 5; awareness-level content from Business Support 12 and 16; Business and Enterprise Support (2013) unit 7.

#### Summary

To be a good business support professional you need a broad, current understanding of the essential areas of business and how they link to and support each other. You also need practical knowledge of the competing demands, pressures and motivations facing the people who run businesses. This Standard sets the level of knowledge expected of every practitioner: enough to recognise issues across the whole business, see how they connect and know when specialist help is needed. The Specialist Standards set out greater depth in particular areas, for example finance (S1), AI and digital adoption (S3), sustainability (S7), people and skills (S8), start-up (S9) and growth and markets (S10).

#### What you need to show

- C6.1 Apply your knowledge of business, its core functions and how they link to and affect each other when working with clients
- C6.2 Apply your understanding of the pressures, motivations and emotional investment involved in running a business
- C6.3 Recognise when a problem presented in one area of the business is caused by another (for example a cash flow problem caused by poor marketing)
- C6.4 Keep your knowledge of business up to date, including changes in regulation, technology and the economy
- C6.5 Recognise when a client needs specialist or regulated advice and refer them to a suitably qualified professional

#### What you need to know and understand

- C6.K1 How to assess where a business stands, what its owners want for it and what it will take to succeed
- C6.K2 How businesses plan, from simple action plans to full business plans
- C6.K3 The core functions of a business (finance, customers and markets, people, operations and technology, sustainability, and legal and regulatory requirements) and how they affect each other, at a level sufficient to recognise issues in each and when specialist help is needed
- C6.K4 How to keep track of regulatory change, where authoritative information is available, and when to refer to a professional adviser
- C6.K5 The needs of different types of business, including the self-employed, home-based, micro, small, medium-sized and family businesses, social enterprises and co-operatives, and structured formats such as franchising, licensing, direct selling and distribution agreements
- C6.K6 What businesses typically need at each stage, from pre-start and start-up through growth and maturity to difficulty, succession and exit
- C6.K7 What running a business asks of the people who do it: the personal stake they have in it, the range of abilities it calls on, the risks they carry, and the time and energy it takes

### C7 Practise ethically and professionally

Evolved from: the ethical and professional guidelines referred to throughout the Business Support Standards (units 1, 2, 3, 5, 6 and 7); Business Support 11 (item h); Business and Enterprise Support (2013), the guidelines referred to in units 5, 10, 13 and 18, and unit 17 (in part).

#### Summary

This is about working to professional and ethical standards in everything you do. It covers integrity and impartiality, managing conflicts of interest, confidentiality and data protection, working within your competence and the limits of regulated activity, inclusive practice, safety and the responsible use of digital and AI tools. In the original standards, these were left to the guidelines of each organisation or professional body. They are now set out as part of the Standards themselves.

#### What you need to show

- C7.1 Act with honesty, integrity and impartiality, putting the client's interests first
- C7.2 Identify, declare and manage conflicts of interest, including commercial interests, referral arrangements and the pressure of programme targets
- C7.3 Keep client information confidential and handle personal data lawfully, securely and only for agreed purposes
- C7.4 Work within the limits of your competence and role, and within the boundaries of regulated activity (for example regulated financial or legal advice)
- C7.5 Treat all clients fairly, work inclusively, and make reasonable adjustments where a client needs them
- C7.6 Recognise ethical dilemmas and matters of professional conduct, and respond to them appropriately, seeking advice where needed
- C7.7 Use digital and AI tools responsibly in your practice: protect client information, check outputs for accuracy, and be open with clients about how you use them
- C7.8 Take reasonable steps to protect your own safety and that of your clients, including when meeting in person, working alone or visiting clients' premises
- C7.9 Follow the policies, codes of conduct and contractual requirements that apply to your work, and raise concerns where they conflict with the client's interests
- C7.10 Check your practice regularly against these Standards, relevant regulations and your professional and ethical obligations

#### What you need to know and understand

- C7.K1 The ethical principles of business support: integrity, impartiality, objectivity, confidentiality, competence and respect
- C7.K2 What counts as a conflict of interest and how to manage one
- C7.K3 The principles of data protection and your obligations when collecting, storing, sharing and deleting client information
- C7.K4 The boundaries of regulated activity relevant to business support (for example regulated financial advice, credit broking, reserved legal activities and insolvency advice), and when to refer
- C7.K5 Equality legislation, inclusive practice and reasonable adjustments
- C7.K6 How AI and other digital tools can create risks to confidentiality, accuracy and fairness, and how to manage them
- C7.K7 Health, safety and personal safety in client work, including lone working and online meetings
- C7.K8 How to respond if you have concerns about the welfare of a client or someone connected with them
- C7.K9 Complaints and whistleblowing procedures, and how to raise concerns
- C7.K10 The professional codes and contractual requirements relevant to your work

### C8 Develop own practice and networks

Evolved from: Business Support 5 (Develop networks to provide access to information, support and resources); Business Support 6 (Evaluate the quality of your own practice in business support); Business Support 7 (Develop your own ability to provide business support); Business Information 5 to 7; Introduction to Enterprise Support 3; Business and Enterprise Support (2013) units 1 (in part), 12 (in part), 13, 17 and 18.

#### Summary

This is about disciplined reflection on your practice, continuing professional development and building the networks that make you a valued professional to work with. Whatever the focus of your practice, your aim should be to keep developing your ability to support clients, from a personal and career point of view and in line with the needs of the organisation you work for.

#### What you need to show

- C8.1 Review from time to time what your role requires of you and how that is changing
- C8.2 Reflect on your practice after client work and at key points, using valid evidence about your methods, their impact and the improvements needed
- C8.3 Seek honest feedback regularly from clients, colleagues and others who see your work
- C8.4 Assess your knowledge, skills and behaviours against these Standards and identify your development needs
- C8.5 Plan, do and record your continuing professional development, revising your plan as your role and practice change
- C8.6 Keep your knowledge of business practice and business support up to date, including developments in technology and policy
- C8.7 Develop professional networks, both formal and informal, that open up information, expertise and opportunities for you and your clients
- C8.8 Give to your networks as well as take from them, so that people trust you, and keep client confidences within them
- C8.9 Open up your contacts to clients where this creates opportunities for them
- C8.10 Evaluate the impact of your development activities and networks on the quality of the support you provide

#### What you need to know and understand

- C8.K1 Why reflective practice matters, how to do it, and why self-assessment alone can mislead
- C8.K2 Sources of useful feedback on your practice (for example professional supervision, peer review, reflective practice groups and client feedback)
- C8.K3 How to write a development plan with clear, measurable objectives and timescales
- C8.K4 The strengths and limits of different ways of developing (for example courses, workshops, conferences, mentoring, peer learning, online learning and work shadowing)
- C8.K5 How you learn best
- C8.K6 How to grow and keep up networks, including through specialist groups, business networks and online professional communities
- C8.K7 How to judge what your networks are worth to you and your clients, against the time they take
- C8.K8 The CPD expectations that apply to you, including those of SFEDI and of your organisation or professional body
- C8.K9 Your own values and motivations for working in business support, and how they affect your practice

### S1 Business Finance and Funding

Who it's for: business support professionals who help clients understand their finances, plan financially and access finance.

Evolved from: Business Support 9 (finance knowledge, items 4 to 9); Business Support 2 (knowledge items 7, 17 and 18); funding content in Business Support 1, 5, 10 and 14.

#### Summary

This is about helping clients understand their financial position, plan and forecast with confidence, and find and secure the right finance for their stage and circumstances. It includes knowing where general business guidance ends and regulated financial advice begins.

#### What you need to show

- S1.1 Help the client understand their financial statements and what they show about the health of the business
- S1.2 Use financial ratios and performance measures to interpret the client's financial information and identify trends, risks and opportunities
- S1.3 Help the client build cash flow forecasts and financial projections, and test the assumptions and scenarios behind them
- S1.4 Help the client understand their costs, pricing, margins and break-even point
- S1.5 Help the client identify their financing needs and the forms of finance suited to their stage, sector and circumstances
- S1.6 Help the client prepare to approach funders, including the plans, information and evidence funders expect
- S1.7 Explain the obligations, costs and risks of different forms of finance, including personal guarantees and giving up equity
- S1.8 Present financial findings and recommendations clearly to support the client's decisions
- S1.9 Recognise the limitations of financial analysis, and when the client needs an accountant, a regulated financial adviser or an insolvency professional
- S1.10 Recognise early signs of financial difficulty and help the client to act early

#### What you need to know and understand

- S1.K1 The profit and loss account, balance sheet and cash flow statement, and the financial terms used by small and medium-sized businesses
- S1.K2 The relationship between profit, cash flow and forecasting, and how to manage working capital, credit control and late payment
- S1.K3 Financial ratios and key performance indicators, and their limitations
- S1.K4 Costing, pricing, margins and break-even analysis
- S1.K5 The main forms of finance (for example grants, loans, overdrafts, asset and invoice finance, government-backed schemes, equity and angel investment, crowdfunding and community finance) and when each is suitable
- S1.K6 What different funders look for, and how to prepare a business for investment
- S1.K7 The basics of business taxation and digital tax requirements, and when to refer to an accountant
- S1.K8 The boundaries of regulated activity in finance (for example regulated financial advice and credit broking) and how to work within them
- S1.K9 The signs of financial distress and the sources of help available
- S1.K10 Financial modelling and forecasting tools, including spreadsheets and accounting software

### S2 Project and Programme Delivery

Who it's for: business support professionals who plan and deliver advisory projects, manage client caseloads, or run business support programmes.

### S4 Enterprise Mentoring

Who it's for: volunteer and professional mentors supporting people who are starting or running businesses.

Evolved from: Business Support 4 (modes of support); Business Support 19 (item j); Business Information 1 (knowledge items 4 and 5, on how clients take in information); Introduction to Enterprise Support 1 to 4; Business and Enterprise Support (2013) unit 20.

#### Summary

This is about supporting the development of a business owner through a mentoring relationship. The mentor brings their own experience and insight; the mentee sets the goals and makes the decisions. It involves contracting the relationship clearly, creating a safe space built on mutual trust, listening and questioning in ways that help the mentee reach their own insights, drawing on experience without imposing solutions, recognising when mentoring has become advice, and bringing the relationship to a planned close once it has served its purpose.

#### What you need to show

Contracting and relationship

- S4.1 Explain to the mentee what mentoring involves, how it differs from advice, coaching and consultancy, and what it asks of them
- S4.2 Set up the relationship with the mentee: its purpose, ground rules, confidentiality, how often and where or how you will meet, how long it is likely to last, and what happens if a meeting cannot go ahead
- S4.3 Create a safe environment and a relationship of mutual trust and respect, taking account of the mentee's culture, background and ways of thinking and working, including neurodiversity
- S4.4 Agree which topics are within the scope of the relationship, and ask permission before exploring personal or sensitive areas

The mentoring conversation

- S4.5 Listen with full attention to what the mentee says and what they leave unsaid, without judging, and check your understanding
- S4.6 Ask well-timed, open and curious questions that help the mentee explore issues and reach their own insights
- S4.7 Recognise the feelings, concerns and pressures behind what the mentee says, and respond with sensitivity
- S4.8 Draw on your own business experience, including mistakes as well as successes, through examples and stories that help the mentee learn, without imposing your own solutions
- S4.9 Offer information and ideas with patience and tact, at a pace and in a form the mentee can absorb and use
- S4.10 Encourage the mentee to use new points of view when evaluating their situation and options

Developing the mentee

- S4.11 Leave the mentee in charge of their own goals and decisions
- S4.12 Build the mentee's confidence, knowledge and skills so they can reach their goals and, in time, manage without a mentor
- S4.13 Notice when you have slipped from mentoring into giving advice, and decide what to do about it
- S4.14 Recognise when the mentee needs support beyond the mentoring relationship and help them access it

Reviewing and concluding

- S4.15 Keep appropriate records of the relationship, in line with data protection requirements
- S4.16 Review progress against the mentee's goals, and the value of the relationship, with the mentee at agreed points
- S4.17 Recognise when the relationship has served its purpose or is no longer adding value, and bring it to a planned close that reviews what has been achieved and agrees next steps
- S4.18 Use supervision or peer support, and feedback from mentees, to reflect on and improve your mentoring practice
- S4.19 Help the mentee look back at setbacks and plans that went wrong, and draw lessons from them

#### What you need to know and understand

- S4.K1 What enterprise mentoring is and its different forms (for example one-to-one, group, peer, reverse and online mentoring; voluntary and paid)
- S4.K2 How mentoring compares with coaching, advising, consultancy and training, and when mentoring is the right form of support
- S4.K3 The stages of a mentoring relationship, from contracting to closure, and how rapport develops as the relationship matures
- S4.K4 Questioning techniques, including open, curious and challenging questions, and how to judge when to use them
- S4.K5 The different levels of listening, from listening to reply to listening for meaning, feelings and what is left unsaid, and how attention and intention affect the quality of listening
- S4.K6 How to recognise and respond to emotions in mentoring conversations, and how stress and pressure can affect business decisions
- S4.K7 How people take in and process information differently, including neurodivergent people, and how to adapt the pace and form of what you share
- S4.K8 How to use your own experience in mentoring: when to share it, how to use stories and examples (including mistakes), and the risk of imposing your own solutions
- S4.K9 Feedback techniques, and the use of reflective practice and supervision in mentoring
- S4.K10 The boundaries of the mentor's role, including how to avoid dependency and how to recognise when a relationship is no longer adding value
- S4.K11 Ethical codes for mentoring, confidentiality, conflicts of interest, and what to do if confidentiality is breached
- S4.K12 Matching mentors and mentees, and what makes a match work
- S4.K13 The practical and personal safety considerations in arranging mentoring meetings, including online
- S4.K14 How to measure the impact of mentoring
- S4.K15 How mentoring programmes are designed, matched and quality assured

Evolved from: new Standard. Builds on Business Support 7 (developing practice in particular business sectors) and Business Support 9 (specific industry sectors and business models).

#### Summary

This is about supporting farms, estates and other rural and land-based businesses in ways that fit how they work. It involves taking a whole-business and whole-family view, starting from a clear baseline, and helping clients make practical, affordable changes, including diversification, responding to changes in farm support and planning for succession.

#### What you need to show

- S15.1 Take a whole-business and, where relevant, whole-family view of the client, including how the household, the land and the business affect each other
- S15.2 Establish a clear baseline of the business's position at the outset, using benchmarking against similar businesses where it is available
- S15.3 Help the client set their own goals and plan changes that are practical, affordable and achievable alongside their working patterns, seasons and capacity
- S15.4 Help the client assess diversification opportunities (for example tourism, food and drink, renewable energy, property and new land uses), including how they fit with the core business and their planning, licensing, tenancy and tax implications
- S15.5 Help the client understand and respond to changes in farm and land-use support and regulation
- S15.6 Help the client assess opportunities in markets for environmental goods and services (for example biodiversity net gain, carbon and nature schemes), including the long-term commitments and risks involved
- S15.7 Help the client overcome constraints common in rural areas, including digital connectivity, transport, access to markets, planning, energy supply and recruiting and housing staff
- S15.8 Use peer learning, discussion groups and practical, on-site learning alongside one-to-one support, where these will help the client
- S15.9 Help the client plan for succession early, including involving the next generation and addressing family and ownership issues
- S15.10 Help the client find and access rural, sector and general sources of finance and support, and keep in contact to help them maintain progress
- S15.11 Help the client work with other local businesses and community organisations, including through collaboration, co-operatives and community-owned enterprises
- S15.12 Recognise signs of isolation, stress or financial pressure, and direct the client to specialist rural support

#### What you need to know and understand

- S15.K1 The structure of the rural economy and the range of rural businesses, including farming, forestry, food and drink, tourism, creative and home-based businesses
- S15.K2 The particular constraints facing rural businesses (for example connectivity, transport, planning, grid capacity, housing, skills and access to finance) and the support available to address them
- S15.K3 The economics of farm and land-based businesses, including enterprise costings, benchmarking, and the effects of seasonality, weather and commodity prices
- S15.K4 Farm and land-use support schemes and regulation, how they are changing, and how to keep up to date with them
- S15.K5 Diversification options and the planning, licensing, tax, insurance and tenancy issues they raise
- S15.K6 Markets for environmental goods and services, and how natural capital can be valued and traded
- S15.K7 Land ownership and tenancy arrangements, and how they affect business decisions
- S15.K8 Family business dynamics and succession planning in farming and other rural businesses
- S15.K9 Effective approaches to rural business support, including peer groups, discussion groups, demonstration and on-farm learning combined with one-to-one support
- S15.K10 Community businesses, co-operatives and other models of rural collaboration
- S15.K11 Sources of specialist rural support, including rural support charities, land agents and sector bodies

### S16 Creative Industries

This is about helping creative businesses, freelancers and cultural organisations to protect and earn from their intellectual property, build sustainable business models, manage irregular income, plan and fund projects, and connect with the networks and specialist support around them.

#### What you need to show

- S16.2 Help the client identify, protect, manage and earn from their intellectual property, including through licensing, royalties and contracts
- S16.3 Help the client develop a sustainable business model, which may combine commissions, projects, products, licensing, teaching, grants and other income
- S16.4 Help freelancers and portfolio workers manage irregular income, set their rates, agree contracts, deal with late payment, and plan for tax and pensions, recognising the unpaid time spent pitching and on administration
- S16.5 Help the client plan and cost creative, research and development, and innovation projects before they apply for funding
- S16.6 Help the client identify and access sector-specific finance, including public funding, investment, crowdfunding and creative industry tax reliefs, and prepare strong applications
- S16.7 Help the client negotiate collaborations, co-productions and commissions, including how intellectual property and income will be shared
- S16.8 Help the client reach audiences and markets, including through digital distribution, platforms and international markets
- S16.9 Help the client assess the opportunities and risks that AI and other new technologies pose for their creative work and their intellectual property
- S16.10 Connect the client with creative networks, clusters, freelancer communities and specialist sector support, building on what already exists locally
- S16.11 Help cultural and not-for-profit creative organisations strengthen their governance, resilience and investment readiness

#### What you need to know and understand

- S16.K2 The role of freelancers and micro businesses in the creative workforce, and the particular pressures they face
- S16.K3 Intellectual property, including copyright, design rights, trade marks, patents and moral rights; licensing; collective management organisations; and how rights are enforced
- S16.K4 How to value and use intangible assets, including when raising finance
- S16.K5 Creative industry funding and finance, including public funders, specialist investors, and creative industry tax reliefs and expenditure credits (for example for film, television, video games, theatre, orchestras and exhibitions)
- S16.K6 Contracts used in creative work, including commissions, licences, fees and the assignment of rights
- S16.K7 Fair work and payment practices for freelancers, and the protections available against late payment
- S16.K8 How AI is changing creative production and distribution, and the current position on copyright and AI
- S16.K9 Audience development and digital distribution
- S16.K10 Creative clusters, networks and specialist support, and how to keep up to date with them
- S16.K11 The barriers to diversity and inclusion in the creative workforce, and how support can address them

### S17 Business Resilience

Who it's for: business support professionals who help businesses prepare for, withstand, recover from and adapt to disruption.

Evolved from: new Standard. Builds on Business Support 2 (knowledge item 8, risk assessment) and Business Support 3 (knowledge item 22, contingency planning). S17 is about preparing for and adapting to disruption; S11 (Supporting Businesses in Difficulty) covers what to do once a business is already in difficulty.

#### Summary

This is about helping businesses understand the risks that matter most to them, put proportionate plans in place that grow with the business, and learn and adapt after every disruption. It covers the operational, financial, digital and people sides of a business, and the personal resilience of the owner.

#### What you need to show

- S17.1 Help the client identify the risks and potential disruptions most relevant to their business, using national, local and sector risk information
- S17.2 Help the client identify their critical activities, people, suppliers, customers, systems and data, and the impact of losing each of them
- S17.3 Help the client develop a business continuity plan, proportionate to the size of the business, that sets out how they would keep critical activities going and recover, starting simply and building over time
- S17.4 Help the client strengthen their financial resilience, including cash reserves, access to credit, appropriate insurance and reducing dependence on a few customers or suppliers
- S17.5 Help the client strengthen the resilience of their supply chain, including alternative suppliers and stock levels
- S17.6 Help the client put basic cyber security and data backup measures in place, and plan how they would respond to and recover from a cyber incident
- S17.7 Help the client prepare for physical risks, including extreme weather, flooding, fire and the loss of power or premises
- S17.8 Help the client reduce their dependence on key people, including through documented processes, cross-training and succession arrangements
- S17.9 Help the client plan how they would communicate with staff, customers, suppliers and others during a disruption
- S17.10 Encourage the client to test and review their plans regularly and after any incident, and to act on the lessons learned
- S17.11 Help the owner build their personal resilience, including managing pressure, workload and isolation, and direct them to wellbeing support where needed
- S17.12 Help the client recognise early signs of longer-term change in their market or environment, and adapt their business model in response

#### What you need to know and understand

- S17.K1 What business resilience means: the ability to anticipate, prepare for, respond to, recover from and adapt to disruption
- S17.K2 The main types of risk facing businesses, and sources of risk information, including national risk assessments and sector research
- S17.K3 Risk assessment and business impact analysis techniques suitable for small businesses
- S17.K4 The principles of business continuity planning, recognised standards and good practice (for example ISO 22301), and how to apply them in proportion to the size of the business
- S17.K5 Financial resilience, including cash buffers, credit facilities, insurance (including business interruption cover) and the risks of depending on a few customers
- S17.K6 Supply chain risk and how to reduce it
- S17.K7 The basic cyber security controls small businesses need (for example backing up data, protecting against malware, keeping devices secure, using strong passwords and recognising phishing) and how to respond to an incident
- S17.K8 Physical and climate-related risks, including flood preparedness, and how businesses can adapt to them
- S17.K9 Key person risk and succession arrangements
- S17.K10 How to communicate during and after a disruption
- S17.K11 Personal resilience and wellbeing for business owners, including the effects of pressure, isolation and fear of failure, and the support available
- S17.K12 How resilience planning links to support for businesses in difficulty (S11), and when to refer

### S18 Developing Enterprising Skills

Who it's for: business support professionals, enterprise coaches, and employability and youth workers who help people develop enterprising skills, whether or not they go on to start a business.

Evolved from: Business and Enterprise Support (2013) unit 19 (Support the client through developing personal enterprising skills) and unit 1 (developing creativity and innovation in others). S18 is about enterprising skills for any path; S9 (Start-up and Self-employment Support) covers starting a business.

#### Summary

This is about helping people develop the attributes, behaviours and skills that make them enterprising: spotting opportunities, generating and testing ideas, thinking creatively, making the most of the resources they already have, and taking measured risks. These skills matter in employment and study as well as in starting a business, so this Standard applies whatever path the individual chooses.

#### What you need to show

- S18.1 Help the individual consider how being enterprising could help them reach their goals, whether in employment, self-employment, study or starting a business
- S18.2 Help the individual take stock of how enterprising they are, recognising strengths and areas to develop
- S18.3 Help the individual spot opportunities and generate ideas, and understand how an opportunity differs from an idea
- S18.4 Encourage the individual to think creatively and try new approaches when developing and testing their ideas
- S18.5 Help the individual list the resources they already have (for example skills, knowledge, contacts, time and money) and work out what more they would need
- S18.6 Help the individual separate their technical or trade abilities from their business management abilities, and plan how to fill any gaps
- S18.7 Help the individual understand what they could put at risk, financially, personally and socially, in pursuing an opportunity, and how to keep that risk in proportion
- S18.8 Help the individual map out the main challenges, actions and milestones between an idea and putting it into action
- S18.9 Connect the individual with people, networks and programmes that can help them build their enterprising skills

#### What you need to know and understand

- S18.K1 What it means to be enterprising, the attributes, behaviours and skills involved, and frameworks that describe them
- S18.K2 Why enterprising skills matter in employment, self-employment and study
- S18.K3 Ways of assessing how enterprising someone is
- S18.K4 How people spot opportunities, where ideas come from, and how to give constructive feedback on an idea
- S18.K5 Techniques for developing creativity and innovation in others
- S18.K6 Approaches to assessing personal and business resources, including starting from the resources a person already has
- S18.K7 The difference between being good at a trade or craft and being able to manage a business
- S18.K8 The financial, personal and social risks of acting on an idea, and how to manage them
- S18.K9 How informal and formal networks help people find the resources they need
- S18.K10 The barriers people face in developing enterprising skills, and the support available

### S19 Business Model and Value Proposition Design

Who it's for: business support professionals who help businesses design, test and improve their business models and value propositions, using visual tools such as canvases.

Evolved from: new Standard. Builds on Business and Enterprise Support (2013) unit 5 (knowledge items 16 to 19, business models and business plans) and unit 19 (item P5, a framework for the building blocks of a business model), and on Business Support 2 (business analysis). S9 and S18 use these tools when starting a business and developing enterprising skills; S19 covers their use in depth, for any business.

#### Summary

This is about helping business owners set out how their business creates, delivers and earns from value, and how well what they offer meets their customers' needs. It involves using visual tools such as the Business Model Canvas and the Value Proposition Canvas to generate and test ideas as well as analyse what already exists, and helping clients become confident using them for themselves.

#### What you need to show

- S19.1 Explain to the client what business model and value proposition tools are for, and how they can help a business of their size and stage
- S19.2 Choose tools suited to the client's situation, purpose and confidence, and adapt how you use them for the client
- S19.3 Help the client set out their current business model on one page, covering their customers, the value they offer, channels, customer relationships, revenue, key resources, activities, partners and costs
- S19.4 Help the client understand their customers' jobs, problems and hoped-for gains, using evidence rather than assumption
- S19.5 Help the client describe how their products and services relieve those problems and create those gains, and judge how well they fit
- S19.6 Use the tools to generate new ideas for products, services, customers and ways of earning revenue, as well as to analyse what already exists
- S19.7 Help the client identify the assumptions their business model and value proposition depend on, and rank them by risk and uncertainty
- S19.8 Help the client design simple, low-cost tests of their riskiest assumptions with real customers, and interpret the results
- S19.9 Help the client compare alternative business models and value propositions, including their costs, risks and sustainability
- S19.10 Help the client turn the agreed model into priorities and actions, and link it to their financial plans
- S19.11 Facilitate the use of the tools with teams, partners or groups, in person and online
- S19.12 Encourage the client to revisit their business model and value proposition as the business and its market change, and build their confidence to use the tools on their own

#### What you need to know and understand

- S19.K1 What a business model and a value proposition are, and how they differ from a business plan
- S19.K2 The building blocks of a business model and how they affect each other
- S19.K3 Recognised visual tools for business model and value proposition design (for example the Business Model Canvas and the Value Proposition Canvas), their strengths and limits, and the terms on which they can be used
- S19.K4 How to understand customer needs, including customer segments, the jobs customers are trying to get done, their problems and the gains they want
- S19.K5 What makes a good fit between a value proposition and customer needs, and how to evidence it
- S19.K6 Techniques for generating new business models and value propositions, including starting from the value created for customers
- S19.K7 How to identify, prioritise and test assumptions (for example customer interviews, prototypes, landing pages and pilot sales), and how to read the evidence
- S19.K8 Common business model patterns (for example subscription, freemium, marketplace, licensing and social enterprise models) and when they fit
- S19.K9 How business model choices affect costs, revenue, cash flow and risk
- S19.K10 How to adapt the tools for social enterprises, the self-employed and very small businesses
- S19.K11 Facilitation techniques for canvas-based workshops, in person and online, including digital whiteboards

### S20 Consultative Advisory Practice

Who it's for: business advisers, account managers and consultants who hold diagnostic and developmental conversations with business owners and leaders, and want to deepen their consultative practice.

Evolved from: Business Support 2 (Help the client to analyse the business and decide the way forward); Business Support 3 (Enable clients to implement changes in their business); Business Support 4 (Encourage clients to act for themselves); Business and Enterprise Support (2013) units 2, 6 and 22. C2 and C3 set out the diagnosis and support for change that every practitioner provides; S20 covers consultative practice in depth. S13 covers delivering a technical professional service to small businesses.

#### Summary

This is about holding conversations that uncover what a business really needs, challenge constructively and lead to actions the client owns. It involves preparing well, questioning and listening in depth, testing assumptions, influencing without taking over, and making sure advice serves both the business and the objectives of the programme it is delivered through.

#### What you need to show

- S20.1 Prepare for each conversation by researching the business, its sector and its history with the service, and planning what you want to find out
- S20.2 Open each conversation with a clear purpose, agree how the time will be used, and invite the client to set out what matters to them
- S20.3 Use questioning, listening and reflecting back to move from the issue the client presents to their underlying needs, constraints and opportunities
- S20.4 Test the client's assumptions and your own, separating what is known from what is believed
- S20.5 Challenge the client constructively, at the right moment and with evidence, while keeping the relationship strong
- S20.6 Recognise when the client is resisting, and explore the reasons behind it rather than pressing your own view
- S20.7 Help the client see the consequences of acting and of not acting, and reach decisions they own
- S20.8 Influence the client towards action using evidence, examples and options, without taking the decision from them
- S20.9 Agree specific actions at the end of each conversation, with who will do what and by when, and confirm them in writing where appropriate
- S20.10 Adapt your consultative style to the client, the issue and the stage of the relationship, moving between advising, facilitating and challenging as needed
- S20.11 Explain to the client how the support fits the objectives of the programme it is delivered through, and make sure your advice serves the client's needs as well as those objectives
- S20.12 Use self-assessment, observation and feedback on your conversations, and reflection, to improve your consultative practice

#### What you need to know and understand

- S20.K1 What makes an advisory approach consultative, and how it differs from providing information or giving instructions
- S20.K2 Structures and planning tools for diagnostic and developmental conversations
- S20.K3 Questioning techniques (for example open, probing, hypothetical, scaling and reflective questions), and how to sequence them
- S20.K4 Levels of listening, and how to listen for meaning, feeling and what is left unsaid
- S20.K5 How to distinguish the issue a client presents from their underlying needs, and the common reasons the two differ
- S20.K6 Approaches to challenge and influence, including the balance between support and challenge, and how to challenge without damaging trust
- S20.K7 How people respond to challenge and change, including defensiveness and resistance, and how to work with them
- S20.K8 Cognitive biases that affect business decisions and advisory judgement (for example confirmation bias and optimism bias)
- S20.K9 Logic models and theories of change in business support, and how individual interventions contribute to programme outcomes and policy objectives
- S20.K10 Tools that support consultative practice (for example question banks, conversation planners, self-assessments and reflective journals)
- S20.K11 How to use observation, peer review and feedback to develop consultative skills, including obtaining consent where conversations are observed or recorded

### S21 Action Learning Facilitation

Who it's for: business support professionals who set up and facilitate action learning sets for business owners, advisers or mentors, and those who use action learning skills in their one-to-one work.

Evolved from: new Standard. Builds on Business Support 19 (Deliver enterprise training to small business clients), Business Support 6 (Evaluate the quality of your own practice in business support) and Business and Enterprise Support (2013) unit 21. S6 covers enterprise training and facilitation in general; S21 covers action learning in depth.

#### Summary

This is about setting up and facilitating action learning sets, in which a small group meets regularly to work on real problems by questioning rather than advising. It includes facilitating in person and online, developing members so that sets can facilitate themselves, developing other facilitators, and using action learning skills in one-to-one work.

#### What you need to show

- S21.1 Explain what action learning is, how it works and what it asks of members, and agree whether it suits the people and the purpose involved
- S21.2 Set up a set, agreeing its purpose, membership, size, how often and how long it will meet, and whether it will meet in person or online
- S21.3 Agree ground rules with the set, including confidentiality, commitment, how time is shared and how members will question rather than advise
- S21.4 Help each presenter set out a real issue that they own and want to act on
- S21.5 Model and encourage open, insightful questions that help the presenter think differently, and steer members away from offering advice or solutions too early
- S21.6 Manage time, energy and participation so that every member presents and contributes
- S21.7 Help each presenter commit to actions, and at the next meeting help them review what they did and what they learned
- S21.8 Facilitate reflection on the process as well as the issues, so that members learn how they learn
- S21.9 Recognise and handle difficulties in a set, such as dominant or silent members, rescuing, advice giving and lapses in commitment
- S21.10 Adapt your facilitation for online and hybrid sets, maintaining participation and trust
- S21.11 Develop members' questioning, listening and facilitation skills so that the set can facilitate itself over time
- S21.12 Use action learning skills, such as questioning, listening and holding back from advice, in your one-to-one advisory and mentoring conversations
- S21.13 Support others to become action learning facilitators, including through co-facilitation, observation and feedback
- S21.14 Evaluate the value of a set to its members and their businesses, and act on what you learn

#### What you need to know and understand

- S21.K1 The principles of action learning, and how it differs from training, mentoring, coaching and other peer groups
- S21.K2 The structure of an action learning set, and the roles of presenter, members and facilitator
- S21.K3 Questioning approaches suited to action learning, and how a genuine question differs from advice phrased as a question
- S21.K4 Listening and reflective practice, and how they support learning from experience
- S21.K5 Group dynamics, and how to build trust and psychological safety in a group
- S21.K6 The common difficulties in sets and how to handle them
- S21.K7 Online and hybrid facilitation techniques and tools
- S21.K8 How to move a set from facilitated to self-facilitated, and when this is appropriate
- S21.K9 How to adapt action learning for business owners with limited time, and for members from different backgrounds and cultures
- S21.K10 How to evaluate action learning, including its effect on members' practice and on their businesses
- S21.K11 Confidentiality and ethics in group work, including what to do if confidentiality is broken

### S22 Business Account Management

Who it's for: business support professionals who manage an ongoing relationship with a portfolio of businesses on behalf of a service, often coordinating support from several teams or organisations around each one.

Evolved from: Business Support 1 (Maintain effective business support relationships with clients); Business Support 10 (Broker clients to business support services); Business Support 11 (Manage a business support client portfolio); Business and Enterprise Support (2013) units 4 and 14. S2 covers managing a caseload within a programme; S22 covers the longer-term relationship with each business and the coordination of support around it.

#### Summary

This is about building a trusted, long-term relationship with each business in a portfolio and making sure the right support reaches it at the right time. It involves account planning, using data and intelligence to understand each business, and coordinating colleagues, partners and specialists as one team around its needs.

#### What you need to show

- S22.1 Agree with the business how the account relationship will work, including the main contacts on both sides, how often you will be in touch and what the business can expect from the service
- S22.2 Build relationships across the business, not only with the owner or chief executive, while respecting who makes decisions
- S22.3 Develop an in-depth understanding of the business's strategy, performance, ambitions, challenges and decision makers
- S22.4 Prepare and keep up to date an account plan with the business, setting out its priorities, the support planned, who is involved and how progress will be reviewed
- S22.5 Use data and intelligence from client records, management information, published sources and conversations to understand each account and anticipate its needs
- S22.6 Coordinate colleagues, partners and specialists as one team around the business, making clear who leads, who does what and how information is shared
- S22.7 Make sure the business experiences joined-up support, without repeated requests for the same information or conflicting advice
- S22.8 Put the business's interests first when deciding what support to offer, including when this does not suit programme targets or your organisation's own services
- S22.9 Prioritise your time and effort across a portfolio of accounts according to need, potential and agreed service levels
- S22.10 Identify opportunities and risks for the business early, and raise them with the business and the right colleagues
- S22.11 Review the value of the relationship with the business regularly, and act on its feedback
- S22.12 Record account activity, contacts and outcomes accurately, so that colleagues can pick up the relationship, in line with data protection requirements
- S22.13 Plan for changes in the relationship, including handover to another account manager and the planned end of account management

#### What you need to know and understand

- S22.K1 The principles of account management and how they apply in business support, including how account management differs from managing a caseload
- S22.K2 Account planning tools and techniques, including mapping relationships and stakeholders within a business
- S22.K3 How businesses make decisions, and the roles and influence of owners, directors, managers and their advisers
- S22.K4 Sources of data and intelligence about businesses (for example client relationship management records, company information, financial data and sector intelligence), and how to analyse them for insight into an account
- S22.K5 How to use client relationship management systems to record, share and review account activity
- S22.K6 How to coordinate support across teams and organisations, including referral agreements, information sharing and consent
- S22.K7 Methods of segmenting and prioritising a portfolio of accounts
- S22.K8 How to judge the health and value of an account relationship, including through client feedback and outcomes
- S22.K9 How to manage conflicts between the business's interests, programme targets and your organisation's commercial interests
- S22.K10 The data protection requirements for holding and sharing information about businesses and the people in them

### S26 Working with Neurodivergent Clients

Who it's for: business advisers, mentors, coaches, trainers and other business support professionals who work with neurodivergent people who are starting or running businesses, and with businesses that employ or work with neurodivergent people.

Evolved from: new Standard. Builds on Business and Enterprise Support (2013) unit 9 (knowledge items 8 and 13, the effects of disability on the client moving from opportunity to action, and the application of learning styles) and unit 20 (knowledge item 17, the learning styles shown by the client). The professional behaviours (B3 and B10) and the Core Standards (C1.K3, C3.K7 and C7.5) set out the inclusive practice every practitioner shows, and S4 covers adapting mentoring; S26 covers working with neurodivergent clients in depth. S27 covers working with disabled clients, and S24 covers reaching groups that are under-represented in business support.

#### Summary

This is about adapting your practice to how each neurodivergent client thinks, communicates and works, building on their strengths, and helping them shape their business around how they work best. It does not involve diagnosing anyone.

#### What you need to show

Adapting your practice

- S26.1 Ask every client how they prefer to communicate, take in information and work, without expecting them to disclose a diagnosis or condition, and act on what they tell you
- S26.2 Adapt how you deliver support and share information, for example by sending agendas and questions in advance, confirming what was agreed in writing, offering shorter or more frequent sessions, giving the client time to process and respond, and using plain language, visual summaries or recordings made with the client's consent
- S26.3 Agree meeting arrangements that suit the client, including the channel, place, time of day and sensory environment (for example lighting, noise and whether cameras are on in online meetings)
- S26.4 Keep anything the client tells you about being neurodivergent confidential, record it only with their agreement and only where it helps you support them, and handle it in line with data protection requirements

Building on the client's strengths

- S26.5 Help the client identify their strengths, interests and ways of working, and how these can give the business an advantage, without assuming that a particular diagnosis brings particular strengths or limits
- S26.6 Help the client shape their business model, role and working patterns around how they work best, including deciding what to do themselves and what to delegate, outsource or automate
- S26.7 Help the client set up practical systems for planning, prioritising, deadlines, money and record-keeping (for example reminders, templates, routines, and assistive and AI tools), and adjust them until they work
- S26.8 Help the client prepare for situations they find harder, such as networking, pitching, negotiating or handling complaints, and find approaches and support that suit them

Wellbeing and wider support

- S26.9 Recognise signs that the client may be overloaded, close to burnout or under strain from hiding their differences (often called masking), talk about it with care, and help them adjust their workload and pace
- S26.10 Help the client decide whether, when and how to tell others, such as customers, funders, partners or staff, that they are neurodivergent, leaving the decision with them
- S26.11 Help the client find and apply for funded workplace support and assistive technology they may be eligible for, including as a self-employed person
- S26.12 Direct the client to assessment services, health professionals, mental health support or specialist organisations, or connect them with peer networks, role models and mentors (including other neurodivergent business owners), as they need and wish

Employers and organisations

- S26.13 Help clients who employ or work with neurodivergent people to understand good practice in recruitment, communication and reasonable adjustments, and refer them to HR or legal advice where needed
- S26.14 Seek feedback from neurodivergent clients on how well your support works for them, and raise with your organisation the parts of its processes, forms, eligibility rules, materials and venues that create barriers, suggesting changes

#### What you need to know and understand

- S26.K1 What neurodiversity and neurodivergence mean; the main forms of neurodivergence (for example autism, ADHD, dyslexia, dyspraxia, dyscalculia and Tourette's syndrome), how often they occur together and how much individuals vary; and the difference between specific learning differences, such as dyslexia, and learning disability
- S26.K2 Respectful language: the different preferences people have (for example identity-first language such as "autistic person" and person-first language such as "person with ADHD"), why to follow each person's own preference, and terms to avoid, such as functioning labels and language that presents neurodivergence as suffering or as a superpower
- S26.K3 The social model of disability, and how the design of services, systems and environments can create barriers for neurodivergent people
- S26.K4 Executive function (for example planning, organising, prioritising, starting tasks, working memory and managing time), how it affects running a business, and practical strategies and tools that help
- S26.K5 Differences in communication, social interaction, sensory processing and information processing, and how to adapt support for them
- S26.K6 The strengths often associated with neurodivergence, the evidence on neurodivergent people in self-employment and business ownership and its limits, and why generalisations about any group can mislead
- S26.K7 Masking, overload and burnout, how they can show, how they relate to mental health, and when to refer
- S26.K8 Routes to assessment and diagnosis, waiting times, and why many neurodivergent people are undiagnosed or self-identify
- S26.K9 Equality legislation as it applies to neurodivergent people, including when a person may meet the legal definition of disability, the duty of service providers to make reasonable adjustments (including anticipating the needs of disabled people), and the duties of employers
- S26.K10 The data protection requirements for information about a person's health or disability, including the extra protection for special category data
- S26.K11 Funded workplace support and assistive technology for neurodivergent people in work and self-employment, specialist organisations and peer networks, and how to keep up to date with changes to schemes
- S26.K12 Good practice for employers in recruiting, managing and supporting neurodivergent staff, at an awareness level, and when to refer

### S27 Working with Disabled Clients

Who it's for: business advisers, mentors, coaches, trainers and other business support professionals who work with disabled people, including people with long-term or fluctuating health conditions, who are starting or running businesses, and with businesses that employ disabled people or serve disabled customers.

Evolved from: new Standard. Builds on Business and Enterprise Support (2013) unit 9 (knowledge item 8, the effects of disability on the client moving from opportunity to action) and unit 5 (knowledge item 26, the business benefits of engaging with equality and diversity), and on the behaviour common to many 2013 units of recognising the opportunities presented by the diversity of people. Making your support accessible

- S27.1 Ask every client what would help them take part fully in the support, without expecting them to disclose an impairment or condition, and act on what they tell you

- S27.8 Help the client understand, at an awareness level, how starting or running a business may affect any disability benefits they receive, and refer them to specialist welfare benefits advice
- S27.9 Help the client find and apply for funded workplace support, equipment, adaptations and assistive technology, including what is available to self-employed people, and plan for the time applications can take
- S27.10 Help the client make their products, services, premises and online presence accessible to disabled customers, and see the market opportunity in doing so
- S27.11 Help the client decide whether, when and how to tell others, such as customers, funders, partners or staff, about an impairment or condition, leaving the decision with them

- S27.13 Help clients who employ, or want to employ, disabled people to understand good practice in recruitment, reasonable adjustments and funded support, and refer them to HR or legal advice where needed
- S27.14 Seek feedback from disabled clients on how accessible your support is, and raise with your organisation the parts of its venues, platforms, materials, processes and eligibility rules that create barriers, suggesting changes

#### What you need to know and understand

- S27.K1 The social model of disability and how it differs from the medical model, and how barriers in services, environments, information, attitudes and systems disable people
- S27.K2 Respectful language, including "disabled people" and "non-disabled people", the terms d/Deaf, British Sign Language user, hard of hearing, blind and sight loss, terms to avoid (for example "the disabled", "handicapped", "wheelchair-bound" and "suffers from"), and why to follow each person's own preference
- S27.K3 The range of impairments and conditions (physical, sensory, mental health, learning disability, and long-term and fluctuating conditions), how many are not visible, and why not to make assumptions from a label
- S27.K4 Equality legislation as it applies across the UK nations, including the legal definition of disability, the duty of service providers to make reasonable adjustments (including anticipating the needs of disabled people), the duties of employers, and the legal recognition of British Sign Language
- S27.K5 Accessibility good practice and standards for venues, events, documents, digital content and online meetings
- S27.K6 How to work with British Sign Language interpreters, other communication professionals, personal assistants and support workers
- S27.K7 How fluctuating and long-term conditions, pain and fatigue can affect running a business, and how to plan for them
- S27.K8 How self-employment can interact with disability benefits, at an awareness level, and where to find specialist welfare benefits advice
- S27.K9 Funded workplace support, equipment, adaptations and assistive technology for disabled people in work and self-employment, and how to keep up to date with changes to schemes
- S27.K10 The data protection requirements for information about a person's health or disability, including the extra protection for special category data
- S27.K11 Disabled customers as a market, and the principles of inclusive design for products, services, premises and online presence

## Introduction to Business Support Standards

The Introduction to Business Support Standards are for people who are thinking of working in business support, or who are new to it. They can be used for self-assessment, introductory and taster programmes, and entry-level learning. The phrase "the people you are working with" can apply to colleagues as well as clients. Evolved from: Introduction to Enterprise Support 1 (Build relationships with others); Business and Enterprise Support (2013) unit 3.

#### Summary

This is about building rapport with people, helping them develop trust and confidence in you, and reviewing how your relationship with them is going. It involves communicating effectively, in person and remotely, so that you can find out what you need to know, listening, reflecting and getting your point across clearly.

#### What you need to show

### E4 Help others to set and realise objectives

- E4.1 Help the person you are working with to set objectives they can realistically achieve
- E4.2 Help them sort what is essential from what would be nice to have, and prioritise
- E4.3 Agree a plan with them that leaves room for them to shape it
- E4.4 Spot what might get in the way
- E4.5 Weigh up the options with them, looking at strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, threats, risks and benefits
- E4.6 Work out what resources they will need and how to get them
- E4.7 Bring in others who can help, and help the person get any further support they need

#### What you need to know and understand

- E4.K1 What the person wants to achieve and what it will take (for example resources, other people's commitment, skills, knowledge and information)
- E4.K2 How to set clear, measurable objectives with timescales
- E4.K3 How to spot and size up the obstacles to progress
- E4.K4 Simple risk assessment and planning techniques
- E4.K5 How to handle the changes that achieving the objectives may bring
- E4.K6 When a need goes beyond what you know, and where to find other support

### E5 Understand how small businesses work

Evolved from: Introduction to Enterprise Support 5 (Knowledge and experience of a small business and its core functions); Business Information 8; Business and Enterprise Support (2013) unit 7 (in part).

#### Summary

If you want to work in business support, you need knowledge or experience of the essential areas of business, including finance, marketing, sales and customer service, and how they link to and support each other. You also need an understanding of the demands, pressures and motivations facing the people who run businesses. This Standard sets that knowledge at an introductory level, as a basis for progressing to the Core Standards (C6 sets the level expected of a business support professional).

#### What you need to show

- E5.1 Apply a basic understanding of how a small business works when helping others
- E5.2 Recognise how the main functions of a business link to and affect each other
- E5.3 Show an understanding of the pressures and motivations of the people who run businesses

#### What you need to know and understand

Finance

- E5.K1 How to work out how much money a business needs, where it can come from and what funders look for
- E5.K2 How money moves in and out of a business, and how to forecast and track it
- E5.K3 Opening and managing a business bank account, and understanding basic financial accounts
- E5.K4 Common accounting terms, and the basics of business tax and record-keeping

Marketing, sales and customer service

- E5.K5 Market research, understanding what customers want, and knowing who the competition is
- E5.K6 Basic marketing and sales techniques and tools, including websites, social media and selling online
- E5.K7 What good customer service looks like and how to check it is being delivered, and the basics of terms of business

Running a business

- E5.K8 The legal forms a business can take, and the basic legal requirements of running one (for example registration, licences, insurance and data protection)
- E5.K9 How digital tools, including AI, are used in running a small business

Small business experience

- E5.K10 How much of themselves people put into their businesses, and the time and energy it takes
- E5.K11 The main parts of a small business and how they depend on each other
- E5.K12 What starting and running a small business well involves, and the pressures that pull owners in different directions
- E5.K13 The range of abilities business owners need, and the risks they take on

### What is new in this edition

- AI and Digital Adoption (S3), Supporting Businesses in Difficulty (S11), Rural and Land-based Business (S15), Creative Industries (S16) and Business Resilience (S17) are new Specialist Standards
- Business Model and Value Proposition Design (S19) is a new Specialist Standard on using visual tools such as canvases to design, test and improve how a business creates and earns from value
- Developing Enterprising Skills (S18) brings forward the 2013 standard on enterprising skills, for people on any path, not only those starting a business
- Brokering Specialist Support (S25) brings together the original standards on assessing and brokering providers' offers, so that C4 can focus on the finding, explaining and referring every practitioner does
- Consultative Advisory Practice (S20), Action Learning Facilitation (S21), Business Account Management (S22), Digital Marketing and Social Media (S23) and Inclusive Engagement with Under-represented Businesses (S24) are new Specialist Standards that give depth to the advisory, facilitation, relationship and outreach skills set out in the Core Standards
- Working with Neurodivergent Clients (S26) and Working with Disabled Clients (S27) are new Specialist Standards that develop the 2013 standards' attention to disability, diversity and learning styles into full Standards on making support accessible and adapting it to how each client thinks, communicates and works
- Ethical and professional practice (C7) is now a Standard in its own right, rather than being left to the guidelines of each organisation
- Reaching and engaging potential clients is now part of the Core Standards (C1)
- The Business Information and Introduction suites keep their original numbering (I1 to I4 and E1 to E5), updated for digital channels, online research and AI, accessibility and data protection
- Measuring and evidencing impact (C5) is drawn together into a single Standard
- Data protection, digital and remote delivery, and the responsible use of AI are built into the Core Standards
- Mentoring and coaching, previously treated as modes of consulting, now have their own Specialist Standards (S4 and S5)
- The personal behaviours repeated in every original unit are set out once (B1 to B11)
- Dated references (for example to Business Link, regional economic strategies, fax and named funding schemes) have been removed, and duplicated units have been merged
